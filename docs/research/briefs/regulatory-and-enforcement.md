**Prepared:** 2026-09-15

## 1. Regulatory Frame for US Credit Card Servicing/Sales Conduct

### 1.1 UDAAP — Dodd-Frank §§1031, 1036 (12 U.S.C. §5531, §5536)

`[P]` Section 1031 of Dodd-Frank gives the CFPB authority to declare acts or practices in connection with a consumer financial product or service **unfair, deceptive, or abusive**. Section 1036 makes it unlawful for a "covered person" (which includes bank card issuers and, per CFPB guidance, their service providers) to commit a UDAAP violation. The CFPB's UDAAP examination manual (most recent public version reissued 2023) sets out the three-part legal tests:
- **Unfair**: causes or is likely to cause substantial injury not reasonably avoidable by the consumer and not outweighed by countervailing benefits.
- **Deceptive**: a representation, omission, or practice likely to mislead a reasonable consumer, and the misleading impression is material to a purchase/use decision.
- **Abusive** (Dodd-Frank-specific, not in FTC Act): materially interferes with a consumer's ability to understand a term or condition, OR takes unreasonable advantage of a consumer's lack of understanding, inability to protect their interests, or reasonable reliance on the covered person to act in the consumer's interest.

`[S]` CFPB Bulletin 2013-07 (used the legal standards above) is still the reference doc practitioners cite for the unfair/deceptive tests being "substantially similar" to FTC Act §5. In Feb 2020 the Bureau issued a (later withdrawn/contested) Statement of Policy narrowing the "abusiveness" standard; regulatory posture on "abusive" has fluctuated across CFPB leadership changes — treat "abusive" as the most legally unsettled of the three prongs `[K]`.

**Design implication**: Nearly every misconduct scenario in this POC (misrepresenting a product as free, enrolling without consent, pressuring a vulnerable customer, exploiting information asymmetry at retention/cancellation) maps most naturally onto UDAAP unfair/deceptive/abusive, and is the *charging theory actually used* in every CFPB card enforcement action found below.

### 1.2 FTC Act §5

`[P]` 15 U.S.C. §45 — prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce." Historically the FTC's primary tool against national banks was limited (banks are largely carved out of FTC jurisdiction under the FTC Act itself), but §5's legal standard is the doctrinal ancestor of UDAAP and is still directly relevant for non-bank affiliates, marketing partners, and third-party product vendors (e.g., the payment-protection product administrators used by card issuers) that are FTC-jurisdictional. `[K]`

### 1.3 TILA / Regulation Z — the operative disclosure and conduct rules

`[P]` Truth in Lending Act (15 U.S.C. §1601 et seq.), implemented by Regulation Z, 12 CFR Part 1026. Key sections for a card-servicing conduct model:

- **§1026.5b** — requirements for open-end credit plans secured by a dwelling (HELOC) — generally not applicable to unsecured cards; included in scope note only because it's sometimes conflated with §1026.6/.9. Not a primary driver for card-servicing misconduct scenarios.
- **§1026.6** — account-opening disclosures (APRs, fees, grace period, balance computation method) — misconduct scenario: agent verbally describes different/better terms than the account's actual disclosed terms (verbal-vs-written mismatch).
- **§1026.9** — "change in terms," including the requirement of at least 45 days' advance written notice before increasing APR, fees, or other significant terms `[K, high confidence]`. Misconduct scenario: agent tells a customer a rate increase is effective immediately/retroactively, or fails to mention the right to reject/opt out of certain changes before the effective date.
- **§1026.13** — billing error resolution — misconduct scenario: agent discourages a customer from submitting a written billing-error dispute, misstates the 60-day dispute window, or resolves a dispute informally without triggering required error-resolution procedures and timelines.
- **§1026.51** — "ability to pay" (CARD Act rule): card issuers **may not open a new credit account or increase a credit limit** without considering the consumer's independent ability to make required minimum payments, based on income or assets and current obligations `[P, confirmed via CFPB text this session]`. Misconduct scenario: retention/upsell agent processes a credit-line increase (CLI) without the required income/ability-to-pay assessment, or coaches the customer to overstate income to get the CLI approved.
- **§1026.56** — over-the-limit transactions: **card issuer may not charge an over-limit fee unless the consumer has affirmatively opted in**, opt-in can be revoked at any time, and no over-limit fee may be charged if the limit was exceeded solely because of fees/interest posted that cycle `[P, confirmed]`. Misconduct scenario: agent enrolls a customer in over-limit fee "protection" without a clear opt-in, or fails to honor a revocation request, or a customer is charged an over-limit fee generated solely by that cycle's finance charges.
- **§1026.60** — disclosures for credit and charge card applications/solicitations (Schumer box) — misconduct scenario: telesales agent verbally describes terms inconsistent with the required tabular disclosure sent/referenced during the offer.
- **CARD Act penalty-fee rules / "Late Fee Rule"** `[P]`: In March 2024 the CFPB finalized a rule lowering the safe-harbor late fee for large issuers (>1M open accounts) from ~$30 (inflation-adjusted, was ~$32/$41 for repeat violations) to **$8**, eliminated the higher repeat-violation safe harbor, and removed annual inflation indexing. Two days after issuance, the Chamber of Commerce, ABA, Consumer Bankers Association and others sued in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas. **On April 15, 2025, the court vacated the rule** via a consent judgment jointly requested by the CFPB and plaintiff trade groups; the CFPB's Chief Legal Officer stated the Bureau agreed the rule violated the CARD Act's "reasonable and proportional" penalty-fee standard. **Practical status as of this research (Sep 2026): the pre-2024 late-fee safe harbors (~$30/$41, inflation-indexed) are back in effect; the $8 cap is dead.** `[P/S — confirmed via Holland & Knight, Goodwin, Consumer Finance Monitor coverage]`. This is a good "policy/offer version drift" scenario generator: synthetic cases can include agents citing a stale/no-longer-effective $8 cap, or conversely citing outdated pre-2024 fee tables during the ~13-month window the $8 rule was nominally in effect.

### 1.4 ECOA / Regulation B (12 CFR Part 1002)

`[P]` Prohibits discrimination in credit transactions on protected bases (race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, receipt of public assistance income, good-faith exercise of Consumer Credit Protection Act rights). Card-relevant mechanics:
- **Spousal signature rule**: a creditor generally may not require the signature of a person other than the applicant (e.g., a spouse) on a credit instrument if the applicant qualifies individually, except in limited circumstances (e.g., using jointly-owned property as collateral, community-property states) `[P]`.
- **Authorized users**: a creditor must report authorized-user account history to consumer reporting agencies in the same name as reported for the primary cardholder when it reports info about the primary holder (relevant to spousal-account credit-building scenarios) `[K, moderate confidence — verify against 12 CFR 1002.10 before use]`.
- **Adverse action notices**: creditor must notify an applicant of action taken within 30 days of a completed application, and provide specific reasons if requested within 60 days `[P, confirmed]`. Also triggered by CFPB/Fed guidance when a card issuer reduces a credit limit based on credit-report information (this is an *FCRA* risk-based-pricing/adverse-action trigger as much as an ECOA one) `[S]`.
- Misconduct scenarios: agent discourages a female or non-English-speaking applicant from applying individually ("just have your husband apply"), fails to send/mention adverse action notice rights when declining a credit-line-increase request, or an agent's script effectively varies credit-line-increase approval likelihood by inferred marital status/age in violation of ECOA's prohibited-basis rules. Fed Reserve compliance outlook flags spousal-signature and adverse-action-notice violations as recurring top Reg B findings `[S]`.

### 1.5 FCRA (Fair Credit Reporting Act, 15 U.S.C. §1681 et seq.)

`[K, standard knowledge, not independently re-verified this session beyond adjacent confirmations]` Key mechanics for the misconduct taxonomy:
- **Hard vs. soft inquiry**: a hard pull (e.g., for a new account or CLI requiring full underwriting) requires a "permissible purpose" and consumer awareness; several enforcement actions below (US Bank 2022, BoA 2023) explicitly cite **use of consumer reports without a permissible purpose** in connection with unauthorized applications as an FCRA violation `[P, confirmed in both consent-order summaries]`.
- Misconduct scenario: agent tells customer "checking this won't affect your credit" when it is in fact a hard inquiry, or pulls a report/opens an account without the customer's request to hit a sales target (directly the fact pattern in the US Bank and Bank of America cases below).
- **Credit reporting statements**: misconduct scenario — collections-adjacent agent threatens to "report this to the credit bureau today" as a pressure tactic inconsistent with actual furnishing practice/timing (an UDAAP/FDCPA-adjacent false-statement pattern).

### 1.6 FDCPA / Regulation F (12 CFR Part 1006) — relevant if in-house or third-party collections voice/chat is in scope

`[P]` Regulation F (effective Nov 30, 2021) requires a written validation notice within 5 days of initial communication (unless already provided), containing itemized debt information, dispute rights, and (new) a tear-off dispute prompt form. Reg F also codified a call-frequency safe harbor (commonly cited as no more than 7 calls per debt per 7-day period, and no call within 7 days of a prior telephone conversation about that debt) `[S — the "7-in-7" figure is widely reported but I did not pull the primary CFR text this session; verify 12 CFR 1006.14(b) before hard-coding into synthetic scenarios]`. Misconduct scenarios: agent fails to provide required validation disclosures orally when required, exceeds call-frequency limits, misrepresents the legal status/amount of a debt, or fails to note/honor a cease-communication or attorney-representation request. **Note**: FDCPA/Reg F applies most cleanly to third-party debt collectors; first-party bank servicing of its own delinquent card debt is primarily governed by UDAAP rather than FDCPA, though CFPB has signaled UDAAP standards for first-party collectors track FDCPA norms closely `[S]`.

### 1.7 TCPA & Telemarketing Sales Rule (TSR)

`[P]` TCPA (47 U.S.C. §227) prohibits autodialed or prerecorded/artificial-voice calls to wireless numbers without **prior express consent** (prior express **written** consent specifically for telemarketing calls). The TSR (16 CFR Part 310, FTC) requires that before obtaining a consumer's consent to pay, the seller/telemarketer must disclose all material terms; recorded verbal consent must clearly capture both the request and the grant of consent and its purpose. `[S]` Recording-consent laws are largely **state-law** (many states are one-party consent, but roughly a dozen, including California and Florida, require all-party consent for call recording) — a federal TCPA/TSR analysis alone is insufficient; state wiretap/eavesdropping statutes are a distinct compliance layer relevant to any inbound/outbound recording pipeline feeding this POC. Misconduct scenarios: outbound retention/upsell call proceeds without confirming/disclosing recording where required by state law, or an agent re-uses "for quality purposes" boilerplate that doesn't function as valid TSR pre-consent disclosure of a paid add-on's material terms.

### 1.8 SCRA (Servicemembers Civil Relief Act) & MLA (Military Lending Act)

`[P/S]` SCRA caps interest (defined broadly to include fees/service charges/renewal fees) at **6%** on debts, including credit cards, incurred **before** the servicemember entered active duty; the excess above 6% must be **forgiven, not deferred**, retroactive to the start of active duty, upon request (with a supporting military order/notice) `[S, confirmed via OCC Comptroller's Handbook: Servicemembers Civil Relief Act and DOJ Servicemembers/Veterans Initiative page]`. Key nuance: some SCRA provisions carry **strict liability** — a good-faith processing error is still a violation, which is unusually punitive relative to most consumer-protection statutes and increases the value of an accurate SCRA-flag detection scenario. MLA (10 U.S.C. §987, DoD's MLA regulation) separately caps the "Military Annual Percentage Rate" at 36% for active-duty servicemembers and dependents and restricts mandatory arbitration clauses and certain fee structures, and generally applies at *origination* rather than being a retroactive-request regime like SCRA. Misconduct scenarios: agent fails to apply the 6% cap retroactively when a valid SCRA request/documentation is received; agent talks a servicemember out of invoking SCRA protections ("that will hurt your credit line" — a common real-world retention-agent misrepresentation pattern per `[K]`); collections agent on a card in SCRA-protected status continues charging above-cap interest or pursuing default-judgment-adjacent contact patterns without required protections.

### 1.9 GLBA (Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act) — privacy & authentication

`[P/S]` GLBA Privacy Rule requires clear, conspicuous privacy notices at account opening and (subject to later Reg P amendments) periodically thereafter; the Safeguards Rule requires access controls including customer-identity verification before disclosing nonpublic personal information. `[S]` Industry practice is knowledge-based authentication (KBA — SSN last 4, DOB, address, security question), one-time passcodes, or verified callback numbers before discussing account details. Misconduct scenarios: agent discloses account balance/transaction detail or makes account changes (address change, add authorized user, PIN reset) **before** completing required authentication (a classic "identity verification skipped under time pressure" QA-flag pattern); agent authenticates a caller using a weaker method than policy requires for a high-risk action (e.g., verifying only name+last-4 before processing an address change that is a common precursor to card-takeover fraud).

### 1.10 State UDAP laws

`[K]` All 50 states plus DC have "mini-FTC Act" UDAP statutes (e.g., California UCL/CLRA, New York GBL §349) that frequently have lower liability thresholds, private rights of action, and are stacked on top of federal UDAAP theory in state AG and class-action litigation against card issuers. Not independently verified this session; flag as background context only — state UDAP claims often ride alongside the federal CFPB theories in parallel litigation (e.g., state AG actions sometimes accompany CFPB card enforcement).

### 1.11 Elder financial exploitation guidance

`[P]` FinCEN Advisory FIN-2022-A002 (June 15, 2022) updates 2011 guidance (FIN-2011-A003) and lists financial and behavioral "red flags" for elder financial exploitation (EFE), instructing institutions to include the term "Elder Financial Exploitation" in SAR narratives and to name staff who witnessed behavioral red flags. `[S]` A 2024 interagency statement (FFIEC-member agencies incl. NCUA press release referenced) reinforces this. Financial red flags include sudden large withdrawals/gift-card purchases, dormant-account activity spikes, wires described as "tech support," "winnings," "taxes." **Card-servicing-specific translation** `[K]`: a caller (possibly not the accountholder) requesting a large credit-line increase, rushing an authorized-user addition, pressuring for expedited card replacement/shipping to a new address, or coaching an elderly cardholder in the background of a call are the analog red-flag patterns for a call-monitoring model — this is a good candidate for a "third-party on the line / caller-not-cardholder" misconduct-adjacent (fraud-adjacent, not agent misconduct, but agent's *failure to escalate* is the misconduct) scenario category.

### 1.12 Language access / LEP guidance

`[P]` CFPB "Statement Regarding the Provision of Financial Products and Services to Consumers with Limited English Proficiency" (Jan 13, 2021), Federal Register 2021-01116. Key points: financial institutions should identify which non-English languages are used by a meaningful share of their customer base (using internal data or Census language data) and are not required to translate into every language but should make risk-based decisions; CFPB confirmed that collecting a customer's language preference does not itself violate ECOA/Reg B. Misconduct scenario: agent proceeds with a complex disclosure (fee waiver terms, settlement offer, APR change) in English to a customer who has indicated a language preference/LEP need without offering interpretation, or a bilingual agent selectively omits required disclosures when switching languages mid-call (harder to catch with English-only QA — a good multilingual-detection scenario, see §5).

## 2. Real Enforcement Actions Against Card Issuers — Facts, Detection, Remediation

All figures below are as reported in CFPB/OCC/DOJ press materials and corroborating secondary coverage found this session; treat exact dollar figures as `[P]` where sourced to a .gov press release/consent order and `[S]` where only trade press was found.

### 2.1 Capital One — 2012 — `[P]` CFPB's first-ever public enforcement action
- **What happened**: CFPB examination found Capital One's third-party call-center vendors used deceptive tactics selling payment protection and credit monitoring add-ons: telling customers they *had* to buy the product to activate their card; enrolling customers without consent (then billing and making cancellation difficult); falsely claiming the products would improve credit scores.
- **Agent-level conduct pattern**: script deviation into high-pressure/false-urgency framing; silent/unconsented enrollment; false credit-score benefit claims.
- **Penalty/remediation**: ~$140M refund to ~2M customers + $25M CFPB penalty; separately $35M OCC penalty + $10M additional refund. Total ~$210M.
- **Detection**: CFPB examination (not whistleblower/complaint-triggered per available summary) uncovered the vendor call-center practices.
- Source: CFPB enforcement page, Cordray remarks, Clifford Chance briefing.

### 2.2 Discover Bank — 2012 — `[P]` Joint FDIC/CFPB action
- **What happened**: Deceptive telemarketing of Payment Protection, Credit Score Tracker, Identity Theft Protection, and Wallet Protection add-ons.
- **Penalty/remediation**: ~$200M refund to 3.5M+ consumers + $14M penalty (total figure reported elsewhere as ~$214M). Independent auditor required to report to CFPB/FDIC on compliance with restitution.
- **Detection**: Originated as an FDIC investigation; CFPB joined in 2011.
- Source: CFPB/FDIC joint press release, NAFCU compliance blog.

### 2.3 American Express — 2012/2017/2025 — `[P]` Multiple actions across a decade
- **2012** (referenced in search results, not deep-dived this session, `[S]`): part of the same wave of add-on-product actions against major issuers alongside Capital One/Discover.
- **2017**: CFPB ordered ~$59.5M refund to 335,000+ consumers for illegal billing/deceptive marketing of add-ons (payment protection, credit monitoring) across three Amex subsidiaries, spanning conduct from **2000–2012** run through subsidiaries, vendors, and telemarketers; Amex paid an additional $9.6M civil penalty; separately, Amex had already voluntarily provided ~$95M of remediation to ~221,932 consumers during the Bureau's review (a genuinely useful "self-remediation before formal order" data point for a POC's remediation-workflow modeling).
- **2025 (Jan 16)**: American Express agreed to pay **$230M** to resolve DOJ and Federal Reserve investigations into **deceptive small-business credit card sales practices**. DOJ's $108.7M portion alleged Amex sales employees offered products (including a "Premium Wire" service) as a scheme to help customers lower taxes/earn extra points, and that employees used **fabricated/"dummy" Employer Identification Numbers** (example cited: "123456788") to open small-business credit card accounts in 2015–H1 2016 — i.e., **falsifying application data to push through sales**, not just mis-selling an add-on. This is a distinct and higher-severity misconduct archetype than the 2012-era add-on cases: **application fraud committed by or coached by front-line/sales employees to hit targets.**
- Source: CFPB newsroom (2017 order), Law360/PYMNTS/CNBC/Payments Dive/Legal Dive (2025 settlement).

### 2.4 Citibank — 2015 — `[P]` $700M, CFPB's largest add-on action found
- **What happened**: From at least 2003–2012, Citibank marketed/enrolled consumers in five debt-protection add-ons (AccountCare, Balance Protector, Credit Protection, Credit Protector, Payment Safeguard) plus identity-theft/credit-monitoring add-ons, with deceptive marketing, billing, and administration. Specific finding: Citibank represented that its credit-monitoring add-on would alert consumers to *fraudulent purchases*, when in fact it only alerted on credit-bureau file changes — a materially false product-capability claim.
- **Penalty/remediation**: ~$700M consumer relief across ~9M accounts + $35M CFPB civil penalty.
- Source: CFPB press release, CFPB consent order PDF, ncontracts analysis.

### 2.5 JPMorgan Chase — 2013 — `[P]` Joint OCC/CFPB
- **What happened**: Unfair billing for identity-theft-protection add-ons — consumers charged for credit-monitoring services **enrolled between Oct 2005–June 2012 but not actually receiving the full benefit** (e.g., billed without the monitoring/reporting actually being performed, a "billed for nothing" pattern distinct from deceptive-sales-at-point-of-enrollment).
- **Scope**: ~2.1M harmed consumers; ~$309M subject to restitution.
- **Penalty**: $20M CFPB civil penalty + $60M OCC civil penalty. Chase had already stopped new enrollments mid-2012 and fully exited the products by end of 2013 (voluntary wind-down predating the order).
- Source: CFPB consent order, OCC news release, JPMorgan Chase IR page.

### 2.6 Bank of America — 2014 — `[P]` Joint OCC-initiated/CFPB-joined
- **What happened**: "Privacy Guard," "Privacy Source," "Privacy Assist" identity-protection add-ons — deceptive marketing (payment-protection products) plus unfair billing (customers charged for credit monitoring/reporting services never actually delivered) for ~1.9M accounts.
- **Scope**: ~1.4M consumers affected by deceptive marketing; ~1.9M accounts billed without service.
- **Penalty/remediation**: ~$727M consumer relief; $20M CFPB penalty + $25M OCC penalty.
- Source: CFPB newsroom, ConsumerAffairs, Consumer Finance Monitor.

### 2.7 US Bank — 2022 — `[P]` Unauthorized/"sham" account opening
- **What happened**: US Bank employees, under sales-goal pressure with sales performance built into job requirements, **opened credit cards, lines of credit, and deposit accounts for customers without their knowledge or consent**, and without required applications/disclosures — violating TILA, Truth in Savings Act, CFPA (UDAAP), and FCRA (pulling credit reports without a permissible purpose for unauthorized applications).
- **Penalty/remediation**: $37.5M CFPB civil penalty; required remediation plan to refund all unlawfully charged fees/costs plus interest.
- **Root cause explicitly named by CFPB**: incentive/sales-goal structure, not just individual rogue agents — i.e., the order frames this as a **management/incentive-design failure**, not purely a front-line conduct failure. Important nuance for a POC: some "misconduct" is systemically induced by quota design, which argues for pairing call-level detection with incentive-structure/branch-level aggregate analytics.
- Source: CFPB newsroom, consent order PDF, Consumer Finance Insights.

### 2.8 Wells Fargo — 2016 (the canonical case, even though not itself a *card-only* action) — `[P]`
- **What happened**: ~1.5M unauthorized deposit accounts and **623,000 unauthorized credit card accounts** opened 2011–2016 to meet cross-sell/sales quotas; employees created fake email addresses to enroll customers in online banking to cover tracks.
- **Penalty**: $185M total ($100M CFPB — then the largest CFPB penalty ever — + $35M OCC + $50M City/County of LA); bank had already terminated 5,300 employees via internal review before the order; refunded $2.6M in related fees.
- **Relevance**: this is the reference case for "quota-driven mass unauthorized account opening" and established the now-standard practice of speech-analytics-based sales-integrity monitoring across the industry (see §4). Also the most widely cited case for "agent behavior was rational given incentive design," reinforcing the same lesson as US Bank 2022.
- Source: NPR, Forbes, CFPB enforcement page, OCC release.

### 2.9 Bank of America — 2023 — `[P]` Compound violation: fees + card enrollment + rewards
- **What happened**: Three distinct misconduct strands bundled into one action: (1) **double-dipping NSF/"double-cycle" fees** — charging multiple insufficient-funds fees on re-presentment of the same unpaid transaction (practice ended Feb 2022); (2) since at least 2012, **employees illegally applied for and enrolled consumers in credit card accounts without knowledge/authorization** (echoes Wells Fargo/US Bank pattern, at a smaller relative scale); (3) **withholding credit card rewards/sign-up bonuses explicitly promised to customers**.
- **Penalty**: $150M total penalties ($90M CFPB + $60M OCC) + $100M additional consumer restitution.
- **Legal theory**: TILA/Reg Z (issuing cards without consent), FCRA (pulling reports without permissible purpose for the unauthorized cards), UDAAP.
- **New misconduct category this case introduces for the taxonomy**: **rewards/bonus non-fulfillment** — agent or system fails to honor a promotional rewards offer a customer qualified for; this is a *servicing* failure distinct from sales-enrollment misconduct and worth its own synthetic scenario bucket (e.g., agent tells customer "you'll get the bonus" but doesn't code the account correctly, or discourages a customer from pursuing a rewards dispute).
- Source: ABC7, CFPB newsroom, Consumer Finance Monitor, NBC News.

### 2.10 Synchrony Bank — 2014 — `[P]` Debt-cancellation add-on + ECOA/language-access violation (dual theory, notable)
- **What happened**: CFPB review of debt-cancellation product telesales found deceptive marketing/enrollment practices (2010–Oct 2012, when telesales was discontinued) — a "twofer" order that **also resolved a separate ECOA violation**: Synchrony **omitted Spanish-speaking customers and Puerto Rico residents from certain statement-credit/settlement offers extended to other similarly-situated customers**, i.e., disparate treatment correlated with language/national origin in an offer-eligibility/retention-offer context.
- **Penalty/remediation**: $56M refund to telesales-enrolled debt-cancellation customers ($11M already refunded pre-order) + $3.5M civil penalty.
- **Relevance for POC**: this is the best-documented real case of **retention/settlement-offer discrimination correlated with LEP/national origin** — directly useful for building a synthetic scenario where an agent (or an offer-eligibility system an agent operates) systematically fails to extend a retention or hardship offer to Spanish-preferring customers.
- Source: CFPB press release, Goodwin ECOA blog post.

### 2.11 Navy Federal Credit Union — 2024 (overdraft, not card sales, but relevant precedent + reversal) — `[P]`
- **What happened**: Illegal "surprise" overdraft fees — charging fees when P2P transfers (Zelle/PayPal/Cash App) showed as available funds at authorization but posted after a cutoff, and when purchases posted after the account had gone negative, sometimes days later (~$44M/year in such fees).
- **Penalty**: $80M+ refund + $15M penalty (Nov 7, 2024) — largest-ever CFPB action against a credit union at the time.
- **Reversal**: On **June 30, 2025**, CFPB's Acting Director (Russell Vought) **terminated the enforcement action** via a two-page order with no stated reason.
- **Relevance for POC — important framing point**: this is a strong real-world example of **enforcement-posture instability** across CFPB leadership changes. A POC's "regulatory risk scoring" should not assume stable enforcement priorities; the same conduct pattern can be a live/reversed case depending on the political environment (see also the Late Fee Rule vacatur, §1.3, and the CFPB's own 2025 posture shifts referenced in supervisory-highlights search results). Build the demo to be **robust to regulation questions being "policy-current-as-of-date-X"**, and consider a version/date field in every synthetic case.
- Source: American Banker, Banking Dive, CFPB archived newsroom, Consumer Federation of America.

### 2.12 Capital One — 2025 — savings-product mis-disclosure, not credit-card sales, but instructive `[P/S]`
- **What happened**: CFPB sued Capital One (Jan 2025) alleging it marketed "360 Savings" as a top-rate account while quietly launching a higher-rate "360 Performance Savings" product without notifying/migrating existing 360 Savings customers, costing them an estimated >$2B in foregone interest as rates rose 2022+.
- **Outcome**: CFPB **dropped its lawsuit in February 2025** (consistent with the broader 2025 pattern of CFPB pulling back — cf. Navy Federal); separately, Capital One settled a **private class action for $425M** (final approval reported), covering customers who held a 360 Savings account between Sept 18, 2019 and June 16, 2025.
- **Relevance**: not a call-center *conduct* case per se (it's a product-design/omission case), but it's a good analog for a **"silent product migration / failure to proactively disclose a better available product"** misconduct category applicable to card servicing too — e.g., an agent who knows a customer qualifies for a better card/lower-rate product or fee waiver but doesn't mention it (arguably not illegal on its own, but a "failure to act in the customer's interest" pattern that maps to the *abusive* UDAAP prong if paired with any information asymmetry exploitation).
- Source: Kiplinger, Virginia Business, NBC News, Axios, NPR.

### 2.14 Cross-case pattern summary (for the misconduct taxonomy)

| Pattern | Cases | UDAAP prong | Detection route (per orders/press) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enrolled/charged without consent | Capital One, Discover, Citi, Synchrony, US Bank, BofA, Wells Fargo | Unfair + Deceptive | Regulatory exam, internal audit, employee terminations |
| Billed for service never delivered | Chase, BofA 2014, Citi (credit-monitoring capability misrep) | Unfair | Exam of vendor billing/administration records |
| False/misleading product claims (credit-score benefit, fraud-alert capability) | Capital One, Discover, Citi | Deceptive | Consumer complaints + exam |

## 3. Common Servicing/Sales Flows Where Misconduct Arises

The following are the operational touchpoints in a card issuer's contact-center workflow map that the enforcement history above and general industry practice `[K]` flag as highest-risk for misconduct. These should be the "scene templates" for synthetic case generation.

1. **Balance transfer offers** — misrepresenting the promotional APR duration, omitting the balance-transfer fee, or failing to disclose when the promo rate reverts to a (much higher) standard purchase APR; also omitting that a balance transfer can affect available credit for new purchases.
2. **Credit line increase (CLI) requests** — processing without an ability-to-pay assessment (§1026.51 risk, §2.7 US Bank pattern), coaching the customer to inflate stated income, or performing a hard pull while telling the customer it's a soft pull / "won't affect your score."
3. **Product change/upgrade/downgrade** — moving a customer to a higher-annual-fee card without clear consent, or a downgrade that silently forfeits accrued rewards/points without disclosure (BofA 2023 rewards-withholding pattern); also "product change" used to avoid a required new-account underwriting/disclosure process that a true new-account opening would trigger.
4. **Add-on products** (payment protection, debt cancellation, credit monitoring/identity theft protection, card/purchase protection) — the single largest historical enforcement category (§2.1–2.6, 2.10): silent enrollment, "required to activate" false framing, non-delivery of promised monitoring, difficulty cancelling.
5. **Retention offers on cancellation** — the agent has strong information asymmetry and incentive (retention metrics) to (a) not offer the *best* available retention deal, (b) discriminatorily withhold offers from LEP/protected-class customers (Synchrony pattern), or (c) talk a customer with a legitimate hardship (including SCRA-eligible servicemembers, §1.8) out of a protection they're entitled to by framing it as harmful ("that will lower your limit/hurt your credit").
6. **Hardship programs** (forbearance, reduced-APR hardship plans, deferred payment) — agent fails to offer an available hardship program, or steers toward a worse-for-consumer option to protect the bank's interest income, or fails to properly document a hardship enrollment leading to inappropriate subsequent late fees/negative reporting.
7. **Fee waivers/refunds** — inconsistent or discriminatory application of discretionary fee-waiver authority; agent promises a waiver and doesn't process it (creates a false-expectation/billing-error scenario under §1026.13); double-charging (BofA 2023 double-cycle-fee pattern) uncorrected despite customer dispute.
8. **Authorized user adds** — processing without proper verification (identity-theft vector), or ECOA-relevant differential reporting practices for authorized users.
9. **Paperless/autopay enrollment** — enrolling without clear consent (dark-pattern-adjacent UDAAP unfair practice), or autopay mis-set (wrong amount/date) causing missed-payment fees/negative reporting through no fault of the customer.
10. **Travel insurance / purchase protection cross-sell** — classic add-on-product misconduct pattern (§2.1–2.6) applied to a travel-specific product; overstating coverage scope.
11. **"Pay over time" / installment plans on existing balances** (e.g., issuer-branded plans converting a purchase or balance into a fixed-fee installment plan) — misrepresenting the plan's finance charge vs. a true low/no-interest claim; failing to disclose the plan fee is effectively an APR-equivalent charge; steering customers into an installment plan that is worse than simply paying down the revolving balance, particularly to at-risk/near-default customers.
12. **Cash advance** — failing to disclose the (typically higher, no-grace-period) cash-advance APR and separate fee before processing what the customer believes is a normal transaction.
13. **Rewards redemption** — devaluing points/miles at redemption without adequate prior disclosure, or an agent discouraging a legitimate redemption to preserve a "breakage" metric.

## 4. Industry Practice: Conduct-Risk Call Monitoring / QA

### 4.1 Sampling and QA scorecards
`[K, standard call-center QA practice, not independently verified this session]` Typical practice at scale: a small random sample of calls per agent per period (often 2–5% in legacy manual QA models) is manually scored against a rubric; **speech-analytics-driven programs shift toward near-100% automated coverage** with human QA reserved for automatically flagged calls, appeals, and calibration sampling. Scorecards commonly separate:
- **Compliance/must-pass items** (binary, zero-tolerance — e.g., "did the agent authenticate the caller before disclosing account info," "was a required disclosure read verbatim/substantively") — a single fail can zero out the whole call regardless of other scores.
- **Soft-skill/quality items** (scaled, e.g., 1–5 — tone, empathy, resolution efficiency) — not compliance-relevant per se but often blended into the same scorecard, which is a common source of "mixing conduct risk with CX quality" confusion in real programs `[K]`.
- **"Misconduct glossary" / red-flag phrase libraries**: real programs maintain curated phrase/pattern libraries (e.g., "it's basically free," "you have to have this to activate your card," "I'll just go ahead and add that for you," "don't worry about the rate — nobody actually pays that," "let me take care of that paperwork for you" used to imply an action without explicit consent) that trigger automated review queues. This is a strong template for a synthetic "red-flag phrase bank" — but real programs closely guard these libraries as IP, so no public example list was found verbatim this session; the phrase examples above are illustrative `[K]`, not sourced from a leaked/published vendor glossary.

### 4.2 Vendors and their compliance-monitoring positioning (per current marketing/trade-press descriptions, `[S]` — vendor self-description, not independently benchmarked)
- **NICE (Enlighten AI / CXone)**: positioned as enterprise-scale, native compliance analytics plus "Enlighten Copilot" generative assistant layered on the same platform.
- **Verint**: enterprise workforce-engagement + compliance speech analytics on one platform, aimed at large regulated institutions.
- **CallMiner**: positioned as strong specifically on compliance analytics depth and customer-journey mapping (Eureka platform).
- **Observe.AI**: explicitly marketed toward **regulated financial services and insurance** contact centers, combining real-time (in-call) and post-call compliance analytics.
- **Level AI**: positioned for mid-market/specialized QA-calibration use cases.
- **Cresta**: positioned around generative-AI agent-assist trained on "top performer" behavior patterns, real-time coaching, enterprise B2B.
- **Common architecture pattern across vendors** `[K]`: ASR transcription → NLU/keyword-and-intent tagging → rule-based and/or ML compliance-flag scoring → case/queue routing to human QA → outcome feedback loop (agent coaching, scorecard, escalation to compliance/legal for suspected violations).

### 4.3 Regulatory/supervisory hooks for QA programs
- `[P]` **OCC Comptroller's Handbook — Corporate and Risk Governance** and related OCC Bulletin 2010-24 ("Guidance on Sound Incentive Compensation Policies," interagency) require that incentive compensation "appropriately balance risk and reward" and not encourage imprudent risk-taking — this is the supervisory hook examiners use to scrutinize sales-goal design (directly cited as root cause in the US Bank 2022 and Wells Fargo cases above). A dedicated "UDAP/UDAAP" Comptroller's Handbook booklet also exists (found: `pub-ch-udap-udaap.pdf`) as the examiner reference for evaluating UDAAP risk in bank sales practices — I did not fully deep-read its contents this session but flag it as the primary examiner-facing text worth pulling in full for a production design (not just this POC brief).
- `[P]` **CFPB Supervisory Highlights** (periodic bulletins) are the best ongoing public source of real (if anonymized) exam findings. **Summer 2024 (Issue 34, July 2024)** covered servicing/collection of consumer debt broadly and specifically flagged **medical credit card** issues: misleading promotions and undue pressure on patients around **deferred-interest** promotional terms pushed by healthcare providers issuing/promoting the cards. In **December 2024**, CFPB announced expanded credit-card oversight measures and signaled plans (from Spring 2025) to publish more granular card interest-rate/fee/rewards data to increase market transparency — useful as a "regulatory data source" reference for a POC that wants to ground synthetic fee/APR values in real market ranges. **Note**: given the CFPB's 2025 pullback pattern evident elsewhere in this research (Navy Federal reversal, Late Fee Rule consent-vacatur, Capital One suit dropped), verify current CFPB supervisory posture/activity level at implementation time rather than assuming continuity from 2024 highlights `[S/flag]`.

## 5. Known Hard Problems for This POC

1. **Rare positives / extreme class imbalance** — true misconduct is (thankfully) a small fraction of real interactions; even in the largest enforcement cases (Wells Fargo, US Bank) the *rate* of misconduct per agent-call was low relative to total call volume, meaning any detector trained/tested on synthetic data must be validated against realistic base rates, and precision at low recall will dominate the practical trade-off (a model that "catches everything" by over-flagging is operationally useless).
2. **ASR errors** — WER in clean audio is now sub-5% for modern systems `[S]`, but call-center audio is rarely clean: telephony bandwidth (8kHz), background noise, cross-talk, accents/dialects, and code-switching all degrade this; a misconduct-relevant phrase mis-transcribed (e.g., "you don't need to" mis-heard as "you do need to") can invert the compliance signal entirely.
3. **Speaker diarization errors** — reported diarization error rates (DER) of **10–20% in spontaneous, overlapping conversation** even for leading systems `[S]`; Word Diarization Error Rate (WDER) specifically measures word-level speaker-attribution mistakes. For a conduct-monitoring use case this is a first-order risk: **misattributing a customer's complaint or the customer's own words to the agent (or vice versa) can flip a compliant call into a flagged violation or hide a real one.** This is arguably the single most consequential ASR-pipeline risk for this specific application (more so than raw WER), because the entire misconduct/no-misconduct judgment often hinges on *who* said a given phrase.
4. **Cross-channel continuity** — a customer may start in chat (with a written record, easier to audit) and continue on a call (harder to audit), or vice versa; a misleading representation made in chat that is "corrected" or reinforced on a follow-up call requires stitching two different data modalities with different fidelity/reliability characteristics into one case, and current commercial platforms vary widely in how well they unify chat+voice+email into one customer-journey compliance record `[K]`.
5. **Policy/offer version drift** — the Late Fee Rule saga (§1.3) is a real, recent, concrete example: for ~13 months (March 2024–April 2025) the "correct" answer to "what's the late-fee safe harbor" was $8; before and after, it's the older inflation-indexed ~$30/$41 figures. A misconduct detector (or its ground-truth labels) must be **date-aware** — the same agent statement can be compliant or non-compliant purely depending on the effective date of the call, and any static "rules library" baked into a model risks becoming silently wrong when a rule reverts (as it literally did here).
6. **Subjective/soft labels** — "abusive" (the Dodd-Frank-specific UDAAP prong, §1.1) is explicitly about exploiting a customer's lack of understanding or reasonable reliance — this is inherently more judgment-laden than a bright-line disclosure-timing violation, and reasonable QA reviewers can disagree on borderline retention-call pressure tactics. Any labeling scheme should probably separate **bright-line/objective violations** (missed required disclosure, no opt-in on file, hard pull without disclosure) from **judgment-call violations** (pressure, framing, omission-by-implication) and expect much lower inter-rater agreement and messier model performance on the latter.
7. **Agent incentive gaming** — once agents (or their managers) know call patterns are monitored, behavior adapts: agents learn to insert compliance "magic words" (reading a disclosure fast/mumbled but technically present) while still executing the substance of a misleading sales pattern — a known cat-and-mouse dynamic in QA programs generally `[K]`. A synthetic dataset that only contains "obviously bad" calls (no disclosure at all) will fail to stress-test a model against this more realistic, harder "technically compliant, substantively deceptive" pattern — worth deliberately including as its own scenario tier.
8. **Multilingual calls (notably Spanish)** — Synchrony 2014 (§2.10) is direct enforcement precedent that Spanish-language/Puerto Rico customer handling is a real historical locus of *disparate* misconduct (offers withheld), not just a transcription-quality problem. On top of the ASR/diarization difficulties (§5.2–5.3, compounded further for Spanish-language or code-switched audio, which are typically less well-represented in vendor ASR training data than English), there's a substantive fairness question: does the compliance-monitoring model itself perform *worse* (lower recall on real violations, or higher false-positive rate) on non-English calls, which would itself risk perpetuating a disparate-treatment pattern at the detection layer instead of just the sales layer.
9. **Ground-truth scarcity for training/eval** — real enforcement orders (§2) describe *aggregate* patterns and settled facts, essentially never individual call transcripts (those are non-public / protected in underlying exam and litigation records), so there is no public corpus of "known-bad" real calls to calibrate a detector against — synthetic data generation is close to the *only* viable path for a POC, which raises the stakes on getting the misconduct taxonomy and phrase/behavior patterns realistic rather than caricatured.

## Non-obvious insights for building a demo

1. **Two enforcement actions (US Bank 2022, Wells Fargo 2016) explicitly frame the root cause as incentive/quota design, not rogue agents.** A demo that only detects "bad agent, bad call" misses the more sophisticated and arguably more realistic story: aggregate the same conduct signal across an agent cohort/team/time-window to surface *systemic* quota-driven patterns, not just individual flagged calls. This is a strong differentiator to build into a POC's dashboard, not just a per-call classifier.
2. **The $8 late-fee rule's rise-and-vacatur (2024→2025) is a gift for a "policy drift" demo scenario.** You can literally generate two versions of the same call script — one where an agent cites the (then-correct) $8 cap in Jan 2025, and one where the same statement is wrong if made in Sept 2025 after vacatur — to demonstrate that your system's ground truth must be date-versioned, and stale hardcoded rules will produce false compliance verdicts. This is a rare case where the "hard problem" (§5.5) has a real, dated, sourceable example rather than a hypothetical.
3. **Application fraud (fabricated EINs, Amex 2025) is a materially different — and more legally serious — misconduct category than mis-selling an add-on.** Most of the historical enforcement wave (2012–2015) is about *deceptive selling of a real, if oversold, product*. The 2025 Amex case is about **employees falsifying underlying application data** to push transactions through. A realistic taxonomy should have a distinct, higher-severity tier for "fabrication/falsification of customer or application data" vs. "misleading sales pitch for a real product" — they likely need different detection signals (structured-data/account-metadata anomalies, not just transcript language).
4. **CFPB enforcement posture is not stable across time — build the demo to show "as of policy-date X" answers, and consider explicitly demoing a scenario where the regulatory answer *changed* underneath a previously-correct call (Navy Federal, Late Fee Rule, Capital One 360 suit).** This is a credible, sourced talking point for stakeholders skeptical of "the AI will just know the rules" — it won't, unless it's kept current, and that's a product feature (a maintained regulatory-rules layer with effective dates), not a one-time training exercise.
5. **Synchrony 2014 is the best real precedent for a *discriminatory retention-offer* scenario (LEP/Puerto Rico customers excluded from settlement offers) — this is a much less obvious misconduct category than "agent said something misleading" and is a good way to show the POC isn't just doing keyword-spotting for red-flag phrases, but can reason about *disparate treatment across otherwise-similar calls* (a comparison-across-calls capability, not a single-call capability).**
6. **Rewards/points non-fulfillment (BofA 2023) is a servicing-side misconduct category that's easy to overlook if you're anchored on "sales misconduct" — it's post-sale, often invisible on the call itself (the agent may say the right thing and the backend just doesn't execute it), meaning some misconduct is not detectable from the conversation transcript alone and requires cross-referencing account/system-of-record data against what was promised on the call. Consider explicitly building a "promise-vs-system-state mismatch" scenario type that requires structured-data grounding, not just transcript analysis.**
7. **Diarization error, not ASR word-error-rate, is probably the single highest-leverage technical risk specific to this use case** — because the entire misconduct/no-misconduct judgment usually hinges on attributing a specific phrase to agent vs. customer (e.g., "I don't want that" said by the customer vs. misattributed to the agent flips the entire verdict). Prioritize diarization-error injection in synthetic ASR-noise modeling over generic transcription-error injection.
8. **"Technically compliant, substantively deceptive" calls (mumbled-but-present disclosures, correct words with misleading tone/pacing/framing) are a known cat-and-mouse pattern in real QA programs and are exactly the case where a naive keyword-based detector fails** — deliberately include a scenario tier where all "required" phrases are present verbatim but the overall interaction is still misleading (e.g., disclosure read at 2x speed immediately followed by a reassuring "but don't worry about that part"), to demonstrate the model does more than compliance-phrase spotting.
9. **The "silent product migration" pattern (Capital One 360 Performance Savings, 2025) — not a call-center case at all, but instructive — suggests a servicing-misconduct category that isn't about anything an agent says wrong on a call, but about an agent's *failure to proactively surface* a better available product/rate/offer the customer qualifies for.** This is a genuinely hard "sin of omission" detection problem: it requires the model to know what the customer *should* have been offered (external ground truth about product eligibility) versus just parsing what was actually said.
10. **Elder financial exploitation in this domain is mostly not "agent misconduct" — it's "agent failure to escalate" when a third party is coaching/pressuring an accountholder on the call, or a caller who isn't the accountholder is directing unusual account changes.** This is a distinct scenario type from all the "agent did something wrong" categories above: the misconduct-adjacent failure is *inaction/non-escalation*, and detecting it requires modeling caller-behavior red flags (background prompting, unusual urgency, third-party financial-jargon coaching) rather than agent-language red flags.
11. **SCRA carries strict liability for a subset of provisions** — meaning a synthetic scenario where an agent makes a good-faith, non-malicious processing error (e.g., forgets to backdate the 6% rate reduction to the servicemember's active-duty start date) should still be labeled as a violation in your ground truth, even though it reads as "clearly not intentional misconduct" — this is a good scenario to explicitly test whether your labeling scheme conflates *intent* with *violation*, which real law frequently does not.
12. **Only a handful of the real cases were detected via customer complaints** (most were routine regulatory examination findings, one — Wells Fargo — via press/whistleblower). This argues against over-indexing a POC's narrative on "we'll catch what complaints miss" and toward the more defensible framing "we systematize what examination-style sampling does today, at much higher coverage" — i.e., position the tool as an always-on examiner, not a complaint-triage tool.
13. **The FDCPA/Reg F "7 calls in 7 days" contact-frequency rule (if in scope for collections-adjacent servicing calls) is a pattern that requires cross-call state** (call count over a rolling window) rather than single-call analysis — another reminder that not all misconduct categories in this taxonomy are single-utterance-detectable; build in at least one scenario type requiring multi-call/session state, not just per-call transcript analysis. **Caveat: I did not independently confirm the exact "7-in-7" figure against 12 CFR §1006.14(b) primary text this session — verify before hard-coding it as a rule.**
14. **The 2012–2015 add-on-product wave and the 2022–2023 unauthorized-account wave are structurally the same misconduct (unconsented enrollment) recurring roughly a decade apart across different products (insurance-like add-ons, then core accounts/cards) — suggesting a demo that frames its misconduct taxonomy around durable *behavioral patterns* (silent enrollment, false urgency, benefit misrepresentation, billing-without-service, discouraged cancellation) rather than product-specific rules will age better and transfer to whatever the next decade's product wave turns out to be.**
15. **Given real vendors' compliance phrase-glossaries are proprietary and none surfaced publicly this session, don't present a synthetic red-flag phrase library as "industry-standard" — present it explicitly as illustrative/hypothesized, and consider building the demo's detection logic around the *underlying regulatory concepts* (consent absent, disclosure timing, benefit misrepresentation) rather than a fixed phrase list, since real misconduct language is idiosyncratic and phrase lists are brittle and quickly gamed (see insight #8).**
